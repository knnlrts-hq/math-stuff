The Bernoulli Numbers

An interactive exploration of one of the most ubiquitous sequences in mathematics — from sums of powers to the Riemann zeta function.

The Sequence

The Bernoulli numbers Bn are rational numbers defined by the generating function t/(et − 1). They vanish for all odd indices greater than 1, and alternate in sign for even indices — a pattern that encodes deep arithmetic information.

Why two conventions? The generating function t/(et − 1) gives B₁ = −½. But the older convention B₁ = +½ arises naturally from Bernoulli's original sum-of-powers formula and from the generating function t·et/(et − 1). The difference is just the sign of a single term — but it has been enough to cause confusion for centuries. Since 2022 Donald Knuth has advocated for the +½ convention.

Faulhaber's Formula

The original motivation: compute the sum 1m + 2m + ··· + nm as a polynomial in n. Bernoulli numbers are the universal coefficients that make this work for any power m. Jacob Bernoulli's delight at discovering this — he could sum the 10th powers of the first 1000 integers in minutes — marks one of mathematics' great eureka moments.

The hidden structure: The sum of m-th powers is always a polynomial in n of degree m + 1. For m = 1 you get the triangular numbers n(n+1)/2. For m = 2 the square pyramidal numbers n(n+1)(2n+1)/6. The Bernoulli numbers are what let you leapfrog from knowing these individual formulas to having a single master formula for all m at once.
Bernoulli's boast: After discovering his formula, Bernoulli wrote that it took him "less than half of a quarter of an hour" to find that the sum of 10th powers of the first 1000 integers equals 91,409,924,241,424,243,424,241,924,242,500. Try setting m = 10 and n = 1000 above to verify!

Akiyama–Tanigawa Triangle

A beautiful algorithm: start with the row 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, … and apply a simple recurrence. The leftmost entry of each new row gives the next Bernoulli number. This "triangle" is to Bernoulli numbers what Pascal's triangle is to binomial coefficients — a computational engine hiding elegant structure.

The recurrence: Set a0,m = 1/(m + 1). Then an+1,m = (m + 1) · (an,m − an,m+1). The Bernoulli numbers appear as an,0 — the leftmost column. Each row "distills" information from the harmonic-like seed sequence into the Bernoulli numbers, via a process closely related to finite differences and Stirling numbers.

Growth & Asymptotics

Although the first few Bernoulli numbers are modest fractions, they grow at a breathtaking rate. The asymptotic formula |B2n| ~ 4√(πn) · (n/(πe))^(2n) shows that the even-indexed Bernoulli numbers eventually dwarf any fixed exponential. The denominators, by contrast, are always very well-behaved.

Connections

Bernoulli numbers sit at a crossroads connecting number theory, analysis, topology, and combinatorics. Their most famous appearance beyond sums of powers is in the values of the Riemann zeta function at even positive integers and at negative integers.

Riemann Zeta at Even Integers

ζ(2n) = (−1)n+1 · B2n · (2π)2n / (2 · (2n)!)

The Basel problem and beyond: Euler's famous result ζ(2) = π²/6 is just the n = 1 case of this formula: B₂ = 1/6, so ζ(2) = (2π)² · (1/6) / (2 · 2!) = π²/6. Every even zeta value is a rational multiple of a power of π, and the Bernoulli numbers supply exactly that rational factor. This is one reason the Bernoulli numbers encode arithmetic information about π itself.

Generating Functions

t / (et − 1) = Σn≥0 Bn · tn / n!

This is the exponential generating function with the B⁻ convention (B₁ = −½).

With the B⁺ convention: t·et / (et − 1) = Σ B⁺n · tn / n!

Why this generating function? The function t/(et − 1) appears naturally when you try to express the Euler–Maclaurin formula — the bridge between sums and integrals — in closed form. It's also the moment-generating function of a certain distribution on [0,1], and its poles at t = 2πik encode the periodicity of the integers under the exponential map.

Trigonometric Series

t · cot(t) = Σn≥0 (−1)n · B2n · (2t)2n / (2n)!

The Laurent expansion of t·cot(t) around t = 0 gives only even-indexed Bernoulli numbers.

Ada Lovelace's algorithm: In 1842, Ada Lovelace wrote what is often called the first computer program: an algorithm for Babbage's Analytical Engine that computed Bernoulli numbers. The Bernoulli numbers thus hold the distinction of being the subject of the first published complex algorithm — the dawn of computer science is woven into this sequence.

Patterns & Properties

The Bernoulli numbers conceal an astonishing density of structure. Here are some of the most important patterns and theorems.

Vanishing Odd Terms

Bn = 0 for all odd n > 1. This follows from the generating function: t/(et−1) + t/2 is an even function of t, so all odd coefficients beyond B₁ vanish. This cuts the sequence in half — only even-indexed values carry information.

Sign Pattern

For even n > 0: Bn is negative when n ≡ 0 (mod 4) and positive when n ≡ 2 (mod 4). Equivalently, (−1)n/2+1·Bn > 0. This alternation is inherited from the alternating signs of ζ(2n) when unwound through the zeta relation.

B₂ = +1/6 (2 mod 4 = 2, +) B₄ = −1/30 (4 mod 4 = 0, −) B₆ = +1/42 (6 mod 4 = 2, +) B₈ = −1/30 (8 mod 4 = 0, −) B₁₀ = +5/66 (10 mod 4 = 2, +)

Von Staudt–Clausen Theorem

The denominator of B2n (in lowest terms) is exactly the product of all primes p such that (p−1) | 2n. This is why the denominators look so structured: denom(B₁₂) = 2·3·5·7·13 = 2730 because p−1 divides 12 for p ∈ {2,3,5,7,13}.

Kummer's Congruences

If p is prime and p−1 does not divide 2n, then B2n/(2n) is p-integral and its residue mod p depends only on 2n mod (p−1). This deep arithmetic regularity connects Bernoulli numbers to p-adic analysis and Iwasawa theory.

Irregular Primes

A prime p is irregular if it divides the numerator of some B2k for 2k ≤ p−3. The first irregular prime is 37 (divides the numerator of B₃₂). Regular primes satisfy Fermat's Last Theorem automatically — irregularity is where the difficulty hides.

Exotic Spheres

The number of exotic differentiable structures on the (4n−1)-sphere involves Bernoulli numbers through the Kervaire–Milnor formula. In dimension 7, there are 28 exotic spheres — and the Bernoulli number B₄ = −1/30 lurks in the computation. Pure number theory shapes the geometry of higher-dimensional spaces.

A personal favorite: The number 691 — the numerator of B₁₂ = −691/2730 — is one of the most famous numbers in mathematics. It appears in Ramanujan's tau function, in the theory of modular forms, and as a "congruence prime" for the discriminant modular form Δ. The seemingly innocuous fraction −691/2730 is a gateway to some of the deepest structures in modern number theory.
